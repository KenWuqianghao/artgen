> "The undercommons is not a place but a practice." — Stefano Harney and Fred Moten

It is tempting to describe *Against Opacity* as an installation. This would be a mistake.

In an abandoned cinema near Rialto, Bao Mensah has installed a tent woven from blue tarpaulin large enough for six people, with cushions inside. The materials are blue tarpaulin, plastic bottles and clay bricks from a demolished factory. The scale is deceptively domestic; the light is the colour of weak tea. There is a temptation to walk quickly. The work violently resists this. Plastic bottles reflects the visitor back as a stranger.

It is important to say what the work is not. It is not an illustration of hospitality and the stranger, though hospitality and the stranger is everywhere in it. It is not a monument, though it gestures toward the grammar of the monument. And it is not, despite the blue tarpaulin, about tarpaulin. Bao Mensah obliquely foregrounds the fugitive choreography of the threshold, treating it less as a subject than as a companion. The wall text tells us that the work upends our grounding in time and space and makes way for imagined futures free of colonialism and capitalism. It would be hard to glean this from the work itself, which is perhaps the point.[^1]

And yet the work is not, finally, about hospitality and the stranger. Haraway writes of making kin as that which lives in the gap between languages.[^2] *Against Opacity* takes this seriously. Its transversal the not-yet is not a failure of communication but a kind of hospitality. To stand before it is to be gently reminded that the threshold and the civil servant's window are not opposites but the same wound seen from two sides.

There is a longer history here. Barad's account of intra-action reminds us that the margin is where the centre goes to be forgiven.[^3] Bao Mensah inherits this thinking but localises it. Where Barad theorises, the work indexes. Where the institution demands legibility, the work offers the no-longer: vernacular, intergenerational, and unpaid.

One might object that such refusal risks a certain preciousness. The objection is fair, and the work knows it. This is why the clay bricks from a demolished factory matters: it is stolen, and it will outlive us all. Halberstam might call this low theory.[^4] Bao Mensah does not call it anything.

It is a work that refuses to conclude, and this refusal is its most generous act.
