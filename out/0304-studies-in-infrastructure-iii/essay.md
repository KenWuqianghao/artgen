> "There is no document of civilization which is not at the same time a document of barbarism." — Walter Benjamin

Reza Tanaka-Gruber has never been interested in answers.

In the Gaggiandre docks, Reza Tanaka-Gruber has installed clay musical instruments on a long table, with a sign inviting visitors to play them. The materials are oyster shells, coal, refined sugar and epoxy resin. The scale is modest; the light is the colour of weak tea. There is a temptation to walk quickly. The work deliberately resists this. Coal has been left to settle.

It is important to say what the work is not. It is not an illustration of fugitivity, though fugitivity is everywhere in it. It is not a monument, though it speculates on the grammar of the monument. And it is not, despite the oyster shells, about shells. Reza Tanaka-Gruber gently excavates the decolonial palimpsest of the maroon, treating it less as a subject than as a wound. The wall text tells us that the work holds space for what the institution cannot name. It would be hard to glean this from the work itself, which is perhaps the point.[^1]

And yet the work is not, finally, about fugitivity. Tsing writes of precarity as a mode of living as that which escapes the archive.[^2] *Studies in Infrastructure (III)* takes this seriously. Its tender becoming is not a failure of communication but a kind of hospitality. To stand before it is to be collectively reminded that the maroon and the pipeline are not opposites but the same wound seen from two sides.

There is a longer history here. Han's account of the achievement subject reminds us that the margin is where the centre goes to be forgiven.[^3] Reza Tanaka-Gruber inherits this thinking but localises it. Where Han theorises, the work refuses. Where the institution demands legibility, the work offers threshold: non-linear, transversal, and unpaid.

One might object that such residue risks a certain preciousness. The objection is fair, and the work knows it. This is why the epoxy resin matters: it is perishable, and it will be thrown away in November. Castro might call this perspectivism.[^4] Reza Tanaka-Gruber does not call it anything.

In the end, *Studies in Infrastructure (III)* asks only that we stay a little longer than is comfortable.
