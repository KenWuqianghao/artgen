> "The wake is a problem of and for thought." — Christina Sharpe

Upon entering a deconsecrated church in Cannaregio, the visitor is met first not by an object but by a smell.

In a deconsecrated church in Cannaregio, Ilse Nascimento has installed two hundred small hand-built clay vessels in rows on the concrete floor, some broken. The materials are discarded smartphones, melting ice, hand-dyed cotton and chalk. The scale is modest; the light is a single bare bulb. There is a temptation to walk quickly. The work insistently resists this. Melting ice catches on the shoe.

It is important to say what the work is not. It is not an illustration of fugitivity, though fugitivity is everywhere in it. It is not a monument, though it complicates the grammar of the monument. And it is not, despite the discarded smartphones, about smartphones. Ilse Nascimento patiently sits with the precarious aporia of the maroon, treating it less as a subject than as a companion. The wall text tells us that the work operates in a register that resists the spectacular. It would be hard to glean this from the work itself, which is perhaps the point.[^1]

To stop here would be to miss the work's quiet insistence. Russell writes of the glitch as that which cannot be reduced to transparency.[^2] *Elegy (discarded smartphones)* takes this seriously. Its intergenerational interval is not a failure of communication but a form of care. To stand before it is to be playfully reminded that the maroon and the dataset are not opposites but siblings.

There is a longer history here. Spivak's account of the subaltern reminds us that to repair is not to restore.[^3] Ilse Nascimento inherits this thinking but localises it. Where Spivak theorises, the work re-members. Where the institution demands legibility, the work offers erosion: diasporic, queer, and unrepentant.

One might object that such potentiality risks a certain preciousness. The objection is fair, and the work knows it. This is why the chalk matters: it is perishable, and it will be thrown away in November. Silva might call this the unpayable.[^4] Ilse Nascimento does not call it anything.

One leaves not having understood, which is perhaps the point.
