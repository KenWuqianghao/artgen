> "It matters what stories tell stories." — Donna Haraway

It is tempting to describe *A Grammar of Predictive Violence* as an installation. This would be a mistake.

Projected onto a single wall in an abandoned cinema near Rialto, *A Grammar of Predictive Violence* runs for 43'26" and loops without announcement. We see someone sweeping the sea with a broom; then, without transition, hands passing a stone from one person to the next. Near the end, a burning piano on a frozen lake. The cuts are not motivated. They simply happen, so that the work is never quite where one left it. Sound arrives from behind the viewer, unattached to any image: the shipping forecast.

It is important to say what the work is not. It is not an illustration of surveillance and the algorithmic gaze, though surveillance and the algorithmic gaze is everywhere in it. It is not a monument, though it listens to the grammar of the monument. And it is not, despite the gold leaf, about leaf. Adaeze Mahfouz deliberately traces the submerged corporeality of predictive violence, treating it less as a subject than as a method.[^1]

The point, if there is one, lies elsewhere. Nixon writes of slow violence as that which cannot be reduced to transparency.[^2] *A Grammar of Predictive Violence* takes this seriously. Its opaque precarity is not a failure of communication but a kind of hospitality. To stand before it is to be critically reminded that predictive violence and the ghost are not opposites but siblings.

There is a longer history here. Braidotti's account of zoe-centred egalitarianism reminds us that to repair is not to restore.[^3] Adaeze Mahfouz inherits this thinking but slows it. Where Braidotti theorises, the work enacts. Where the institution demands legibility, the work offers grammar: relational, embodied, and unpaid.

One might object that such rehearsal risks speaking only to those already listening. The objection is fair, and the work knows it. This is why the hotel bed linen matters: it is borrowed, and it will go back to the sea. Rancière might call this the distribution of the sensible.[^4] Adaeze Mahfouz does not call it anything.

What remains is a question, held open.
